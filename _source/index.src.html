Preliminary results. Manuscript in preparation. Advisor: David Wilder, Rutgers University.

Time flies when you’re having zen.Eight minutes of meditation, remembered as five or six.

In 2019, 214 Rutgers undergraduates listened to an eight-minute recording: either a guided meditation or a passage of The Hobbit audiobook. Afterward, with no warning that the question was coming, they estimated how long it had lasted.

This year I reanalyzed the original data from the raw files in R, adding effect sizes, Bayes factors and a full set of robustness checks. The main result held up, but two of my original explanations for it did not.

The design

Each participant was randomly assigned to one of four groups. The recording varied, and so did where attention was pointed: at the self and body, or at a narrative.

54% of meditators chose 5–6 minutes. 70% of audiobook listeners chose the correct 7–8 minutes or longer, compared with 37% of meditators. Cohen's d = −0.63, 95% CI [−0.91, −0.36].

What the reanalysis found

Each result is reported with how strongly the data support it. A Bayes factor compares how likely the data are with and without an effect, so it can show evidence for nothing being there, which a p-value cannot.

How strong is the evidence for each result?

Bayes factors on a log scale. Left of 1 favors no effect, right of 1 favors an effect. Between 1/3 and 3 the data can't decide.

Meditation made eight minutes feel shorter

Strong evidence

Meditators' estimates centered on 5–6 minutes, and audiobook listeners' on the correct 7–8. The difference was medium to large (d = −0.63; F(1, 210) = 23.13, p < .001), and the data are about 5,200 times more likely if the effect is real than if it isn't. It appeared under both kinds of focus. It looked larger with the narrative focus, but that difference wasn't reliable, so I don't interpret it.

Self-awareness did not explain it

Moderate evidence of no link

The original hypothesis was that meditation raises self-awareness, and self-awareness stretches time. Body-focused meditation did raise state self-awareness a little. But self-awareness had no relationship with the duration estimates: r = .00, 95% CI [−.14, .13]. The Bayes factor favors no link by about 6 to 1. The mirror, meant to raise self-awareness without meditation, had no effect.

A smaller echo in seconds

Inconclusive

Before and after the recording, participants timed four shapes shown on screen for 7, 17, 37 and 57 seconds. After meditating, their estimates fell further: to 80.7% of the true durations, compared with 87.4% after the audiobook, once each person's first estimates are taken into account (p = .016). The Bayes factor is 2.4, which can't decide either way, so the paper treats this result as secondary.

How it was done

Software
R 4.6.1 with haven, nlme, BayesFactor and ggplot2
Reproducible
One script rebuilds every number and figure from the raw SPSS files. Each result line states the value it should produce, so any change that breaks a number shows up at once.
Analysis
I ran the full analysis myself in RStudio, working from the raw SPSS files, and checked every result against the original output.
AI-assisted

About

I review human subjects research at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, about 150 submissions a month across behavioral and biomedical studies. Before that I spent two years in Columbia's Department of Anesthesiology, coordinating studies of postoperative delirium and recovery of consciousness.

My research interest is how morally and existentially significant experiences, from meditation to awe to psychedelic states, reshape cognition, identity and the sense of time. I hold a certificate in interdisciplinary psychedelic studies from Ohio State.

Outside work I shoot photos, play guitar, snowboard and scuba dive. If there’s a Phish show or a Grateful Dead cover band nearby, I’m probably there.
